Introduction

Introduction to the Excision Plate Engineering Experiments

 

The Excision‑Inking Plate Experiments (EPE) are a series of tests designed to explore how complex vinyl and hybrid composite laminates can be engineered into printing plates suitable for gotographs—the term I use for plates made from PVC‑based laminates. The intention of these tests is to find dynamic new ways to making printing plates from products that are often considered to be cheap and nasty: placemats, floor vinyl and other complex plastic laminates.

Because these materials rarely behave ideally in their raw state, many require modification before they can be cut. Most of the experiments therefore focus on altering or replacing the upper surface layer.

An ideal gotograph has a fully hydrophobic and oleophobic upper surface, while the layers revealed through excision must remain ink‑absorbent. The EPEs investigate how to suppress the negative behaviours of these materials while amplifying their strengths.

As the experiments progressed, new methods emerged organically—changes in adhesives, pressing sequences, curing strategies, or layer combinations. Each significant shift became a new test series with its own code, allowing the results to be tracked and compared.

 Through this process, my lab assistant Lottie and I identified five major categories of excision‑plate engineering. Each represents a distinct approach to modifying the upper layer, with its own advantages, limitations, and characteristic failure modes:

 

  1. SKIN LAYER EXCISION-PLATE ENGINEERING(SLEPE)
    This first experiment involves what we refer to as “SLEPs”: Skin Layer Excision Plates.
    Basically, in this experiment we have been testing various chemical substances poured onto “vinyl composite laminates” with a porous upper layer, i.e. the cushioned layer of floor vinyl or the absorbant layer of EVA foam, and then pressed with various types of “pressing plates” that they would not stick to during curing. An ideal result is a substance that will form a new “skin” on the laminate which can then be cut through and “excised” in an operation that results in the clean peeling of the shape from the surface, revealing the foamy layer underneath which will absorb the ink. In this ideal situation, the cured upper layer that will remain unexcised will be entirely hydrophobic and oleophobic but will stay attached to the plate during the “excision inking” which involves the vigorous process of pressing excision ink into the fissures with either oil-based or acrylic printing ink (all hand made).
    Test fluids include the following: a paste made from pmma and acetone, melted hybrid pmma and xylene paste (“xlurry”), metal primer, hybrid PVA composite paste, acetone all-purpose glue & tacky glue.
    Results that follow will reveal that although no “ideal” plate was created, nonetheless new plates and methods have resulted that have required new forms of cutting and ink application.

  2. Adhesive Sheet Excision Plate Engineering (ASEPE)
    This second set of tests involves what are called “ASEPs”: Adhesive Sheet Excision Plates.
    This second set of tests involves the adhesion of a “sticky” layer to the top of the vinyl composite laminate. Although many of these adhesive sheets are sticky themselves, it was assumed that additional adhesion would be required to ensure a complete and reliable bond, and this assumption was born out in the tests, i.e. that the additional adhesives we choose would attach to the adhesive rather than the glossy new upper layer itself that would later be excised. In addition, tests were performed on surfaces that were “made sticky” using a sheet of extremely sticky double-sided labels.
    In an ideal situation, a perfect ASEP would be made from large rolls of the sticky sheet in question (so that the ideal size could be predetermined), and that this sheet would be so tightly bonded to the upper layer of the vinyl laminate that it would be torn off with the upper layer of the substrate pre-lamination, revealing the ink absorbent surface underneath. The adhesive sheet itself glossy would remain entirely hydrophobic and oleophobic.
    Results proved that this was possible if difficult to achieve with some of the sheets tested. ASEPs, although not always reliable or long-lasting as some of the other greater successes in these experiments, were absolutely possible to achieve.

  3. Foil Layer Excision Plate Engineering
    This third set of tests results in “FLEPs”: Foil Layer Excision Plates.
    Foil, naturally hydrophobic and oleophobic, proved itself to be a fascinating part of this experiment as we quickly realized while testing cardboard-backed membranes that had foil in them, that this would be a fruitful area to explore on its own. There are actually a lot of different types of surfaces that use foils, complex laminates in their own right which had additional layers that were absolutely wet to adhesives. With the right combination, it is certainly possible to create various types of FSEPs and new methods of plate-making and inking were developed specifically to incorporate these types of plates.
    Various types of foil sheets  used to date include: mirror/holographic foil paper (attached via the tint), window reflective foil, PTE Gift wrapping foil, foil attached to paper and kitchen foil.

  4. Membrane-Sealed Excision Plate Engineering (including Toner-Embedded variants)
    This fourth set of tests, arguably the most fascinating of them all, results in the engineering of  “MSEPs” (and TEMSEPs): Membrane-Sealed Excision Plates and Toner-Embedded Membrane-Sealed Excision Plates.
    The most complex of the tests, this area of testing involved the development of a method to transfer the  partially porous to ink  “membrane” layer of glossy photo paper to vinyl composite laminates. Using various substances including metal primer, PVA hybrid and Acrylic wood varnish, the paper was laid face-down on the adhesive and allowed to cure, and then the resin-coated cardboard backing was carefull removed after applying warm water revealing an entirely flat surface that was either entirely waterproof or ink absorbant in truly unique ways that would gradually release the ink print by print in an ethereal sheen for multiple snatches.
    Furthermore, it was discovered that images printed with a laser printer would also transfer, acting as a “trace” excision inkers could use to guide their cutting as they gradually removed the upper layer using new methods and cutting knives experimented with specifically for this purpose, resulting in the development of new methods of creating pick and peelable excision plates.
    The results? Remarkable. Currently attempts are being made to achieve the much whispered about “IJEMSEP” but always believed to be impossible: the Inkjet-Embedded Membrane-Sealed Excision Plate”.
    Who knows what the future holds?

  5. Fusion-Sealed Excision Plate Engineering
    This set of tests results in the engineering of “FSEPs” (and TEFSEPs), Fusion-Sealed Excision Plates and by extension Toner-Embedded Foil-Sealed Excision Plates.
    Although these tests have really just begin, there have been successful tests with fusion seals in the form of T-Shirt transfers which are attached to the a vinyl laminate using either an iron, a laminator or a heat press (using metal plates, clamps and a heating gun). In addition, Hot-Melt adhesive which requires quite high temperatures to melt is currently being tested on various surfaces. Since it is possible to print “traces” onto t-shirt transfers, TEFSEPs are not only possible but achievable, although to date not FFEPs or TEFSEPs have actually been cut or inked (although a number of them are currently waiting for testing.
